Machine Consumption Governance
Governing Admissibility Rather Than Capability
Mitchell D. McPhetridge
Abstract
Contemporary governance discussions frequently begin with an unnecessarily difficult question:
What should machine systems be allowed to do?
This framing requires regulators to define machine intelligence, anticipate future capabilities, construct universal behavioral rules, and continually revise those rules as technology changes.
The resulting governance burden expands faster than the systems being governed.
This paper proposes a narrower approach.
Rather than governing machine capabilities, governance should focus on the relationships between resource owners and machine consumers.
The central claim is simple:
Governance scales best when it regulates the admissibility of machine participation rather than the capabilities of machine systems.
Content hosts should retain authority over the conditions under which machine consumers may acquire resources.
Machine consumers should possess verifiable identities and operate through explicit permission structures.
Central authority should remain limited to identity integrity, enforcement, fraud prevention, and coordination failures.
The framework treats governance primarily as a problem of admissibility, accountability, and resource relationships rather than intelligence control.
 
⸻
 
Governance Architecture
The framework consists of three foundational governance layers.
Layer	Function
Resource Authority	Declares permissions and participation conditions
Identity	Establishes accountable machine participation
Enforcement	Resolves violations and preserves compliance
These layers are connected through provenance and auditability mechanisms that distinguish acquisition from downstream use.
The framework governs participation rather than intelligence.
 
⸻
 
Core Proposition
Capability governance asks:
What can machines do?
Participation governance asks:
Under what conditions may machines participate?
The framework argues that governance scales best when it regulates admissibility of participation rather than capability of machine systems.
 
⸻
 
Definitions
Machine Consumer
A machine consumer is any automated system that acquires, indexes, retrieves, summarizes, transforms, stores, redistributes, trains upon, or otherwise operationally consumes resources on behalf of itself or another actor.
This definition is intentionally technology-agnostic.
Crawlers, search engines, retrieval systems, language models, autonomous agents, recommendation systems, browser automation tools, distributed machine networks, and future machine architectures may all qualify as machine consumers.
The framework is not tied to any specific generation of technology.
Admissible Participation
Admissible participation refers to machine participation occurring under declared permissions, identifiable relationships, and enforceable conditions.
The framework governs admissible participation.
It does not claim to eliminate unauthorized participation.
Governance systems rarely prevent all violations.
Their purpose is to establish observable permissions, accountability structures, and enforceable boundaries for legitimate participation.
Actors operating outside those boundaries become enforcement concerns rather than permission-governance concerns.
Resource
A resource is any information-bearing, computational, communicative, or operational asset capable of being acquired, consumed, transformed, indexed, summarized, retrieved, redistributed, trained upon, or otherwise utilized by a machine consumer.
Resources may include:
	•	webpages,
	•	repositories,
	•	databases,
	•	APIs,
	•	software archives,
	•	publications,
	•	media assets,
	•	user-generated content,
	•	data streams,
	•	model outputs,
	•	sensor feeds,
	•	and future machine-consumable information structures.
The definition is intentionally technology-neutral.
The framework governs participation relationships surrounding resources rather than specific resource types.
 
⸻
 
1. The Scope Error
Many governance proposals begin by asking:
What should machine systems be allowed to do?
This question immediately creates several difficulties.
Machine systems differ.
Uses differ.
Jurisdictions differ.
Capabilities evolve.
Incentives change.
The governance structure must therefore expand continuously to remain relevant.
The problem grows faster than the solution.
The result is recursive governance expansion.
A narrower question exists:
Under what conditions should machine consumers be permitted to access resources?
This question concerns participation rather than capability.
Participation is observable.
Capabilities are open-ended.
Governance scales more effectively when it operates at the participation boundary.
 
⸻
 
2. The Internet Precedent
The internet did not scale through universal content regulation.
It scaled through local authority combined with shared protocols.
Individual resource owners retained authority over:
	•	access,
	•	participation,
	•	service conditions,
	•	usage policies.
Protocols provided:
	•	identity,
	•	interoperability,
	•	authentication,
	•	routing,
	•	enforcement support.
Local decisions remained local.
Shared infrastructure remained constrained.
Machine-consumption governance can follow the same architecture.
 
⸻
 
3. Governing Relationships
The core governance unit is not the machine.
The core governance unit is the relationship between:
	•	resource owner,
	•	machine consumer.
Capabilities change.
Relationships remain comparatively stable.
Governance therefore attaches to the relationship.
The relevant question becomes:
Under what conditions may this machine consumer participate in this resource relationship?
This structure remains applicable regardless of future technological developments.
 
⸻
 
4. Layer One: Resource Authority
Authority begins with the resource owner.
Repositories, publishers, archives, institutions, creators, and websites should be capable of declaring machine-consumption permissions.
Examples include:
Crawl Rights
	•	Allowed
	•	Restricted
	•	Prohibited
Indexing Rights
	•	Permitted
	•	Restricted
	•	Prohibited
Summarization Rights
	•	Permitted
	•	Attribution Required
	•	Restricted
Training Rights
	•	Permitted
	•	Conditional
	•	Prohibited
Commercial Rights
	•	Permitted
	•	Licensed
	•	Restricted
Attribution Requirements
	•	Citation Required
	•	Source Linkage Required
	•	Repository Acknowledgment Required
Update Permissions
	•	Access Frequency
	•	Synchronization Intervals
	•	API Requirements
The resource owner establishes conditions.
Machine participation becomes conditional rather than assumed.
 
⸻
 
5. Resource Authority and Public Interest Resources
The framework assumes resource owners possess authority to establish participation conditions.
This assumption is generally appropriate for privately controlled resources.
However, some resources possess broader public-interest characteristics, including:
	•	public records,
	•	government publications,
	•	public-domain archives,
	•	publicly funded scientific outputs,
	•	legally mandated disclosures.
In such cases, resource authority may be constrained by public-access obligations.
The framework therefore distinguishes between:
Private Resource Authority
Resource owners determine participation conditions subject to applicable law.
Public Interest Resources
Participation conditions may be constrained by statutory access requirements, public-interest obligations, or public-domain status.
The framework does not assume unlimited resource authority.
It assumes that participation governance operates within the legal status already governing the resource.
Admissibility governance regulates participation.
It does not determine ownership.
 
⸻
 
5.1 Relationship to Existing Law
The framework does not create ownership rights.
It does not expand copyright.
It does not establish new property claims.
It does not alter privacy obligations.
It does not determine the legal status of resources.
Instead, the framework organizes machine participation around rights, obligations, permissions, and restrictions that already exist under applicable legal systems.
Permission governance operates downstream of existing legal authority.
The framework governs admissibility of participation rather than ownership itself.
 
⸻
 
5.2 Open Ecosystems
The framework is compatible with open and permissionless environments.
Examples include:
	•	public-domain archives,
	•	open datasets,
	•	peer-to-peer networks,
	•	decentralized repositories,
	•	open scientific resources.
Open participation does not eliminate governance.
It changes the participation conditions.
An open resource may declare:
	•	unrestricted access,
	•	unrestricted indexing,
	•	unrestricted summarization,
	•	unrestricted training.
Identity, provenance, attribution, accountability, and enforcement mechanisms may still remain relevant even when access restrictions are minimal.
The framework therefore applies to both restricted and open participation environments.
The distinction lies in the declared conditions rather than the existence of governance itself.
 
⸻
 
6. Access, Permission, Contract, and Use
These concepts are related but distinct.
Access
The ability to acquire a resource.
Permission
The declared conditions governing access.
Contract
The obligations attached to permission.
Use
Actions performed after acquisition.
Examples include:
	•	Crawling permitted.
	•	Training prohibited.
	•	Indexing permitted.
	•	Commercial redistribution prohibited.
	•	Summarization permitted.
	•	Dataset incorporation prohibited.
The existence of access permissions does not imply unrestricted downstream use.
Governance therefore requires separate treatment of acquisition rights and utilization rights.
Failure to distinguish these categories creates ambiguity and weakens enforcement.
 
⸻
 
7. Governance Beyond Licensing
Licensing is one component of participation governance.
The framework is broader than licensing alone.
Licensing concerns permission.
Governance concerns the conditions under which participation remains admissible.
These conditions may include:
	•	identity verification,
	•	provenance requirements,
	•	auditability,
	•	compliance monitoring,
	•	fraud prevention,
	•	dispute resolution,
	•	enforcement mechanisms.
A machine consumer may participate under a non-commercial or unrestricted license while still remaining subject to accountability requirements.
The framework therefore governs participation relationships rather than licensing arrangements alone.
 
⸻
 
8. Layer Two: Identity
Permission structures require identity.
Machine consumers should present:
	•	verifiable identity,
	•	organizational affiliation,
	•	declared purpose,
	•	permission scope,
	•	compliance status.
Identity need not emerge from a single authority.
A federated trust architecture is sufficient.
Under such a model:
	•	multiple credential providers may exist,
	•	resource owners determine acceptable providers,
	•	trust remains locally configurable.
The objective is accountability rather than centralization.
Machine consumers become identifiable participants rather than anonymous extractors.
 
⸻
 
9. Permission Handshake
Access begins through explicit negotiation.
The process is straightforward:
	1.	Machine identifies itself.
	2.	Resource owner declares terms.
	3.	Machine accepts terms.
	4.	Permissions are granted.
	5.	Compliance remains observable.
The purpose is not restriction.
The purpose is admissibility.
Participation becomes governed rather than assumed.
 
⸻
 
10. Provenance and Auditability
Certain permissions require post-acquisition verification.
Training rights are the clearest example.
A machine consumer may obtain content under one permission structure while later employ it under another.
Governance therefore benefits from:
	•	provenance records,
	•	acquisition logs,
	•	audit trails,
	•	disclosure requirements,
	•	cryptographic attestations,
	•	dataset manifests,
	•	signed acquisition records,
	•	third-party audits,
	•	dataset accountability mechanisms.
The objective is not perfect surveillance.
The objective is verifiable compliance.
Accountability requires observable history.
Provenance functions as the bridge between acquisition and use.
Without provenance, downstream compliance becomes difficult to establish.
 
⸻
 
11. Governance Economics
All governance systems impose costs.
These may include:
	•	credential issuance,
	•	identity verification,
	•	provenance maintenance,
	•	audit procedures,
	•	dispute resolution,
	•	enforcement activities.
The framework does not eliminate governance costs.
It relocates them.
Capability-based governance requires continuous reinterpretation as technologies evolve.
Participation governance concentrates costs around identifiable events:
	•	acquisition,
	•	permission negotiation,
	•	provenance verification,
	•	compliance disputes.
These costs scale more predictably than capability classification and behavioral prediction.
 
⸻
 
12. Layer Three: Enforcement
Once permissions and identities exist, enforcement becomes possible.
Violations become measurable events.
Examples include:
	•	identity spoofing,
	•	unauthorized crawling,
	•	prohibited training,
	•	attribution removal,
	•	access violations,
	•	contractual noncompliance.
Without declared permissions, enforcement remains ambiguous.
With declared permissions, violation becomes identifiable.
The governance problem shifts from interpretation to verification.
 
⸻
 
13. Central Authority
A central authority remains necessary.
Its scope should remain narrow.
Primary responsibilities include:
Identity Integrity
Preventing fraudulent representation.
Unauthorized Access
Addressing deliberate circumvention.
Copyright Violations
Resolving protected-content disputes.
Fraud
Preventing deceptive machine behavior.
Market Abuse
Addressing anti-competitive conduct.
Systemic Failures
Managing coordination failures beyond local authority.
The central authority governs enforceability rather than machine behavior itself.
 
⸻
 
14. Why This Scales
Capability governance requires continuous adaptation.
Relationship governance does not.
The framework assumes:
	•	new machine architectures,
	•	new retrieval systems,
	•	new agents,
	•	new consumption patterns.
Rather than regulating future capabilities directly, governance regulates participation conditions.
The question remains stable even as technology changes:
Under what conditions may this consumer participate in this resource relationship?
Stable questions scale better than moving targets.
 
⸻
 
15. Constraint Rather Than Prohibition
The framework does not oppose machine participation.
Machine consumers perform valuable functions:
	•	indexing,
	•	retrieval,
	•	preservation,
	•	discoverability,
	•	citation graph construction,
	•	summarization,
	•	propagation.
The objective is not prohibition.
The objective is conditional continuation.
Participation remains admissible while conditions remain satisfied.
Governance therefore operates through constraint rather than exclusion.
 
⸻
 
16. Limitations and Failure Modes
The framework does not eliminate unauthorized participation.
It does not guarantee perfect attribution.
It does not solve international jurisdiction conflicts.
It does not prevent identity fraud.
It does not eliminate enforcement costs.
The framework assumes that some machine consumers will refuse participation entirely.
Such actors remain enforcement concerns rather than permission-governance participants.
The objective is not universal compliance.
The objective is creating observable, enforceable, and scalable participation structures for legitimate machine consumption.
 
⸻
 
17. Conclusion
Machine-consumption governance may be suffering from a scope problem.
The dominant question remains:
What should machine systems be allowed to do?
A narrower and more tractable question is:
Under what conditions should machine consumers be permitted to participate in resource relationships?
This shift relocates governance from capability control to admissibility governance.
Resource owners retain authority over local resources.
Machine consumers become accountable participants.
Identity establishes participation.
Provenance establishes accountability.
Enforcement preserves compliance.
The framework does not attempt to predict future machine behavior.
It governs the conditions under which participation occurs.
The internet scaled through local authority and constrained protocols.
Machine-consumption governance may scale through the same principle.
Governance works best when it governs relationships rather than capabilities, participation rather than intelligence, and admissibility rather than prediction.
